    Tutorial · 5 minutes

    How to find a site where a data center’s heat gets used

    ThermaLite treats every 2 km square of a state as a possible home for a new 50 MW data center, scores it, and shows which nearby buildings would buy its waste heat.

    Go straight to the map
    1. Choose a mission target

      Pick Virginia, Texas or North Carolina at the top left. The map shows the whole state as small squares; the box above the presets tells you how many squares could sell their heat at today’s energy prices.

    2. Pick a mission profile

      Presets set what matters. Heat reuse first hunts for sites beside greenhouses, hospitals and apartments. Protect water steers away from stressed rivers. Clean and cool favors clean grids and cool weather. Balanced weighs everything equally.

    3. Read the map

      Colors run from deep purple (weaker) to bright yellow (better), relative to this state; the legend shows the score range. Dark grey squares are ruled out (flood zone, protected land or water). White dots are data centers that already exist. Gold numbered rings are the top 10 places to build.

    4. Hover and zoom

      Move over the map to read any square’s coordinates and score in the top-left readout. Scroll or use + and − to zoom, drag to pan, and ⤢ to fit the state again.

    5. Open a site report

      Click a ringed site or any square. The report shows its score on each factor with the real number behind it, and, if its heat would sell, a heat delivery plan: teal lines on the map to each buyer, with MWh per year and pipe length.

    6. Fine-tune and compare

      Drag the sliders to change any weight from 0 (ignore) to 5 (matters most). Watch the top 10 move: that is the trade-off between heat reuse, water and carbon, made visible.

    What every output means

    Site score (0–1)

    The weighted average of the five factor scores. Higher is better under your current weights.

    Heat reuse

    Heat a 50 MW data center here could sell each year, after heat pump physics, seasons and pipe cost. Scores 1.0 at 60,000 MWh/yr.

    Clean power

    Based on the state grid’s CO₂ per MWh. Lower emissions score higher. Same value across a state.

    Water stress

    WRI Aqueduct’s baseline water stress, 0 (low) to 5 (extreme). Less stress scores higher.

    Cool climate

    Cooling degree days a year. Fewer hot days means more free cooling and a higher score.

    Grid access

    Distance to the nearest substation. Scores 1.0 next to one and 0 at 15 km or more.

    Heat delivery plan

    Each buyer that would take this site’s heat, how many MWh a year, and the pipe length. The engine fills the best-paying buyers first until the heat runs out.

    Homes’ worth

    Heat reused ÷ 17.9 MWh, the heat per home in Odense, Denmark. A sense of scale, not houses connected.

    About the mission

    Build the next data center where its heat gets used

    Every watt a server uses comes out as heat, and today almost all of it is vented. In Denmark, Sweden and Finland that heat warms tens of thousands of homes. ThermaLite asks where the same thing could work in the US.

    What we found

    Retrofitting today’s data centers mostly doesn’t pay: only 7 of 413 we tested in Washington, Oregon and Texas could sell their heat, all in downtown Seattle. They were built where land and power are cheap, far from heat users.

    So we flipped the question: where should the next data center go? Across Virginia, Texas and North Carolina, ThermaLite found 40 independent sites, about 2 GW of capacity, where a new data center’s heat would have a buyer. That is roughly a third of the 6 GW under construction across the US at the end of 2025. Land isn’t the limit; heat buyers are.

    How it works

    • Grid. Each state is cut into 2 km squares, about 240,000 across the three states.
    • Rule out. Squares more than half flood zone (GLOFAS 100-year), protected land (WDPA) or water and wetland (MODIS).
    • Heat reuse. For each square, a 50 MW data center with chilled-water cooling (heat at about 30 °C) is matched to hospitals, greenhouses, indoor pools and large apartment buildings within 3 km. A heat pump model, each building’s month-by-month demand from 10 years of ERA5-Land climate data, and pipe cost at the state’s EIA prices decide who would buy, and how much.
    • Other factors. State grid CO₂ (EPA eGRID 2023), water stress (WRI Aqueduct 4.0), cooling degree days (ERA5-Land) and distance to the nearest substation (OpenStreetMap).
    • Score. Each factor becomes 0–1 on one fixed scale for every state, and your weights combine them.

    Limits

    • A screening tool: it ranks places worth a real engineering study, not parcels ready to buy. Land, zoning and fiber aren’t checked.
    • Pipe and heat pump costs are placeholder engineering values.
    • Most reusable heat goes to large greenhouses mapped in OpenStreetMap. Some unnamed ones, especially on Virginia’s Eastern Shore, may be poultry houses and need checking on aerial imagery.
    • Grid CO₂ is one value per state, not site by site.
    • Sized for a 50 MW data center. A 500 MW campus makes ten times the heat, more than any single buyer can take.
