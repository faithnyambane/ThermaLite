    Guide

    How to find a site where a data center’s heat gets used

    ThermaLite treats every 2 km square of a state as a possible home for a new 50 MW data center. It scores each square and shows which nearby buildings would buy the waste heat.

    Open the map

    Step by step

    1. Choose a stateVirginia, Texas or North Carolina. The summary on the right shows how many squares could sell their heat at today’s energy prices.
    2. Set your priorityHeat reuse looks for sites beside greenhouses, hospitals and apartments. Protect water avoids stressed rivers and aquifers. Clean and cool favors clean grids and cool weather. Balanced weighs everything equally.
    3. Read the mapDarker green squares score higher; paler squares score lower. Grey squares are ruled out. Small dark squares are data centers that already exist. Numbered markers are the top 10 places to build.
    4. ExploreHover over any square to see its score. Scroll or use + and − to zoom, drag to move, and ⤢ to see the whole state.
    5. Open a site reportClick a numbered marker, a square, or a row in the top 10. The report breaks the score into its five factors and, where the heat would sell, lists each buyer with the heat it would take and the pipe length. Amber lines on the map lead to each buyer.
    6. Fine-tuneChoose Adjust weights to set each factor from 0 (ignore) to 5 (matters most). The ranking updates as you move the sliders.

    What each output means

    Site score
    A 0–1 weighted average of the five factor scores under your current weights. Higher is better.
    Heat reuse
    The heat a 50 MW data center here could sell each year to buildings within 3 km, after heat pump physics, seasonal demand and pipe cost. Scores 1.0 at 60,000 MWh a year.
    Clean power
    The state grid’s CO₂ per MWh (EPA eGRID 2023). One value for the whole state.
    Water stress
    WRI Aqueduct baseline water stress, from 0 (low) to 5 (extremely high). Lower stress scores higher.
    Cool climate
    Cooling degree days a year (ERA5-Land, 2015–2024). Fewer hot days means more free cooling.
    Grid access
    Distance to the nearest substation. Scores 1.0 next to one and 0 at 15 km or more.
    Ruled out
    More than half the square is a 100-year flood zone, protected land, or water and wetland.
    Heat delivery plan
    Each building that would buy this site’s heat, the MWh a year it would take, and the pipe length. The best-paying buyers are filled first until the heat runs out.
    Homes’ worth
    Heat reused divided by 17.9 MWh, the heat per home in Odense, Denmark. A sense of scale, not homes connected.

    About

    Build the next data center where its heat gets used

    Almost all the electricity a data center uses becomes heat, and today it is vented. In Denmark, Sweden and Finland that heat warms tens of thousands of homes. ThermaLite shows where the same idea can work in the United States.

    What we found

    Retrofitting today’s data centers rarely pays. Only 7 of the 413 we tested in Washington, Oregon and Texas could sell their heat, all in downtown Seattle. Most were built where land and power are cheap, far from anyone who needs heat.

    So we asked where the next one should go. We screened six states with favorable energy prices and ranked them by the heat new data centers could sell at their ten best sites that share no buyer.

    How it works

    • Each state is divided into 2 km squares, about 240,000 across the three states.
    • Squares more than half flood zone (GLOFAS 100-year), protected land (WDPA) or water and wetland (MODIS) are ruled out.
    • For every square, a 50 MW data center with chilled-water cooling (heat at about 30 °C) is matched with hospitals, greenhouses, indoor pools and large apartment buildings within 3 km. A heat pump model, each building’s monthly demand from ten years of ERA5-Land climate data, and pipe cost at the state’s EIA energy prices decide who would buy, and how much.
    • Grid CO₂ (EPA eGRID 2023), water stress (WRI Aqueduct 4.0), cooling degree days (ERA5-Land) and substation distance (OpenStreetMap) complete the score. Every factor uses one fixed 0–1 scale for all states.

    Limitations

    • A screening tool. It ranks areas worth a real engineering study; land ownership, zoning and fiber aren’t checked.
    • Pipe and heat pump costs are placeholder engineering values.
    • Most reusable heat goes to large greenhouses mapped in OpenStreetMap. Some unnamed ones, especially on Virginia’s Eastern Shore, may be poultry houses and need checking on aerial imagery.
    • Grid CO₂ is one value per state.
    • Sized for a 50 MW data center. A 500 MW campus makes ten times the heat, more than any single buyer can take.
